# Case Study: Enterprise Agile Transformation at State Farm

**Company:** State Farm  
**Role:** Business Analyst | Scrum Master  
**Timeline:** Feb 2017 – Apr 2022  
**Type:** Agile Transformation · Change Management · Insurance · Delivery Excellence

---

## The Problem

State Farm's claims and policy administration organization was running on Waterfall. A 52-person department, multiple interdependent teams, and a delivery model that was producing software in 6–9 month release cycles. By the time features shipped, business requirements had changed. Defect rates were high. Developer morale was low. And the business was losing confidence in the technology team's ability to respond to market changes.

Leadership had mandated an Agile transformation. But a mandate isn't a plan - and previous transformation attempts at the organization had stalled because they treated Agile as a process change rather than a culture change.

---

## My Role

I was brought in as Business Analyst and Scrum Master with accountability for both the delivery framework and the requirements quality that would make it work.

**Transformation Strategy**
- Assessed the current state across all 4 teams: delivery cadence, defect rates, team confidence scores, and stakeholder satisfaction
- Designed a phased transformation roadmap: team-level Scrum adoption first, then cross-team coordination, then organizational scaling
- Chose not to implement SAFe immediately — the overhead would have crushed adoption before it started; started with pure Scrum at the team level and introduced coordination mechanisms as teams matured

**Scrum Implementation**
- Trained and coached 52 team members on Scrum theory, ceremonies, and artifacts — not just the mechanics but the reasoning behind them
- Established Definition of Ready and Definition of Done standards that gave teams clear quality gates without bureaucratic overhead
- Facilitated all Scrum ceremonies (sprint planning, daily standups, reviews, retrospectives) until team Scrum Masters were ready to own them independently
- Introduced burndown charts and velocity tracking — gave teams visibility into their own performance for the first time

**Cross-Team Coordination**
- Built a Scrum-of-Scrums framework to manage dependencies between the claims team and policy administration team — two groups whose work was tightly coupled but had previously operated in complete silos
- Created a dependency mapping process that surfaced cross-team blockers 2 sprints ahead of when they would become problems, eliminating the last-minute firefighting that had been consuming senior developer time
- Identified and removed cross-team impediments that were collectively costing an estimated 14 hours of developer time per month

**Kanban for Support**
- Recognized that the maintenance and support team's work pattern didn't fit Scrum — unpredictable inflow, no fixed sprint scope
- Implemented Kanban for that team specifically: visualized the work, set WIP limits, established a service-level agreement for ticket resolution
- Result: 35% reduction in average ticket resolution time within 90 days

**Backlog Quality**
- Collaborated with Product Owners to enforce backlog prioritization discipline — ensuring the top 20% of highest-value features were always ready for the next sprint
- Rewrote user story standards for the organization: clear problem statement, specific acceptance criteria, testable outcomes
- Coached junior BAs on requirements quality — reducing the rework caused by ambiguous stories that had previously been a major source of sprint failure

## Lessons Learned

**Transformation fails when it's imposed, not adopted.** The teams that resisted longest were the ones who felt Agile was being done *to* them. The breakthrough came when I shifted from trainer to coach — asking teams what was slowing them down rather than telling them what to do differently. Ownership of the process was the missing ingredient.

**Kanban and Scrum are not competitors.** One of the best decisions of the transformation was recognizing that the support team needed Kanban, not Scrum. Forcing Scrum on a team with interrupt-driven work would have failed and poisoned the broader transformation. Match the method to the work pattern, not the other way around.

**Metrics create accountability without micromanagement.** Before velocity tracking, nobody — including the teams themselves — knew if they were getting better. Introducing burndown charts and velocity wasn't about surveillance; it was about giving teams a mirror. Once they could see their own performance, they started self-correcting without being managed into it.

**The backlog is the product.** The single highest-leverage change wasn't any ceremony or framework — it was improving the quality of user stories. Bad requirements create rework. Rework destroys velocity. Velocity destroys morale. Everything starts with a well-written story.
